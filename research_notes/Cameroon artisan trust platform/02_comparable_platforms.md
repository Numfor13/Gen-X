# Comparable and Competing Platforms: Artisan, Tradesperson and Informal-Worker Matching (Africa-first, global comparables)

Research date: 12 November 2026. "Status" labels give the latest dated evidence found, not a guarantee of current operation. Research note: the shared web-search budget ran out partway through this task. Later items were checked by fetching primary pages directly (company pages, Wikipedia, PDFs extracted locally), so some planned lookups were not done (NFX/a16z essays, Habitissimo, Zaarly, Amazon Home Services' fate, Jiji Cameroon). ## Q1. Cameroon and Francophone/Central African players

### Takeaway
No Cameroonian artisan-matching platform has public traction numbers, funding beyond grant level, or a track record. The space is full of very early apps: Nofey (2025), Maibeta (TEF-funded, 2026), and a "FixIt" app mentioned without detail. Generic classifieds (CoinAfrique, Kerawa, Expat.com) and word of mouth do the real matching. Elsewhere in Francophone Africa (Abidjan, Dakar, Libreville, Lomé) there are similar thin, recent players. The most useful design signals come from Gabon's OZOservir (field acquisition, a cheap verified-badge subscription, 3–4% commission) and Senegal's Whooti (document checks, a flat fee per contract instead of an ongoing commission).

### Cited Findings
**Cameroon**
- **Nofey** (founded 2025; CEO Audrey Ludivine Baha, a Cameroonian agro-industry engineer): a mobile app connecting individuals with artisans and technicians (plumbing, electrical, mechanics). Trust depends on in-app reviews and ratings. Payment is by Mobile Money inside the app, with in-app messaging. The article gives no traction, funding, verification process or city. Article dated 4 Feb 2026. Status: early stage, as of Feb 2026 — [1]
- **Maibeta** (founder Bamai Namata): an on-demand marketplace in Cameroon for verified technicians (electrical, plumbing, carpentry, construction, home maintenance, corporate facility management). It has automated job matching, a structured provider workflow and digital payments. Funding: US$5,000 TEF seed grant. No traction disclosed. TEF alumni feature dated 10 Sep 2026, promotional. Status: early stage, as of Sep 2026 — [2]
- The TEF feature describes the Cameroonian pain points: inconsistent pricing, limited accountability, uncertainty about quality, reliance on personal recommendations, and artisans with no digital track record who depend on word of mouth — [2]
- **"FixIt" (Cameroon)**: a search summary mentions an app connecting qualified plumbers and electricians with clients in Cameroon. I could not verify its operator, launch date or traction — [3] (search-result reference only; low confidence)
- **Kerawa.com**: Douala-based general classifieds (cars, property, jobs, services). Reported to have merged with Afrimalin and Leportail.ci in 2018. It has no dedicated, verified artisan vertical — [3][4]
- **CoinAfrique Cameroon** has a "Services" category that includes "Travaux, bricolage et jardinage", "Ménage & Cuisine" and "Babysitting et nounou". Its trust features are limited to safety tips ("don't pay before checking", meet in public places) and a "Top annonces" badge. The page mentions no seller verification, ratings or buyer protection — [5]
- **Jumia** suspended its e-commerce operations in Cameroon in November 2019. It said it would keep only the classifieds portals (formerly Jumia Deals) there — [6]
- **Jiji Cameroon**: the jiji.cm domain did not resolve (DNS ENOTFOUND, Nov 2026). Jiji's presence in Cameroon is unconfirmed — [7] (Jiji.ng reference)
- Other Cameroonian platforms found are in adjacent sectors (taxi: Klandoo; agri: Jangolo; e-health: Clinic Home), which shows a Douala/Yaoundé app ecosystem but no scaled services marketplace — [8]

**Côte d'Ivoire**
- **Gombo** (Abidjan, launched 2021): iOS/Android app matching clients with artisans and other providers (electricians, sports coaches). Clients are expected to leave a review after each job — [9][10]
- **Djooman.ci**: construction and renovation. Its in-house experts build a custom quote and propose registered artisans. It was web-only, with no app, as of 2024 — [11][12]
- **Artisan CI**: geolocation app. Artisans sign up free "in two clicks" using only a phone number, so there is minimal verification — [13]
- **Ayieko Côte d'Ivoire**: users post projects and receive free quotes from providers (a quote-comparison model) — [14]

**Senegal**
- **Whooti** (domestic workers: nannies, housekeepers, drivers; built by Amentys): staff check each provider's documents and validate the file before listing, and phone numbers are verified. Profiles show experience, references and availability. Contracts are signed in-app. Pricing: employers pay a one-time 10,000 FCFA per contract. Providers register free, but 2,000 FCFA is deducted from their first salary, with no ongoing commission. Orange Money integration is in progress. It reports 678 providers and 98 employers, but the same article also says "more than 1,000 providers" without reconciling the two. Article dated 2 Oct 2026 — [15]
- Other Dakar apps: **Niafey** (home-services booking) [16]; **Khidme** (claims artisans "verified and rated by the community") [17]; **Bridger**, **Allo Jade** and **Xarmat** appear as App Store listings only — [18]
- A search summary attributes these claims to **Tagaba** (launched 2021): a 20% commission and 80,000 users, plus a "35% leakage via WhatsApp after first contact" estimate. On fetch, the Kolonell page did not contain these figures. Kolonell is a vendor selling marketplace builds and cites no sources. It promotes a 12–15% commission per job as the "West African standard", 5,000–15,000 FCFA/month pro subscriptions, Wave/Orange Money escrow, and claims escrow cuts disputes by about 45%. All of these are **unverified vendor claims** — [19]

**Gabon / Togo**
- **OZOservir** (Libreville; founder Béni-Jaël Mbata, diaspora entrepreneur in France): official launch planned for end of Q1 2026, with about 500 pre-registrations. Free sign-up first, then 1,900–5,000 FCFA/month subscriptions (the "Pro" tier gives a verified badge, statistics and a showcase), sponsored placement, and an estimated 3–4% commission on integrated payments. It plans KYC profile verification and verified ratings and reviews, plus geolocation and in-app chat. Acquisition is entirely in the field (markets, neighbourhoods, demos, QR codes) with no media budget. Live status not confirmed — [20]
- **ConsoHub** (Gabon): an app listing artisans, merchants and local providers, built by a Paris-based diaspora business analyst. Year and traction not given — [21]
- **Trankyl** (Togo): web and mobile platform connecting service providers with clients — [22]

### Inferences
- Cameroon has no incumbent with network effects. The real competitor is the status quo (personal referral, WhatsApp/Facebook groups, quartier reputation) plus free classifieds. Being early is an advantage, but the many failed or thin peers show that launching an app does not solve the trust problem.
- Francophone founders are converging on mobile money, ratings and reviews, and a "verified" badge. Few describe *how* verification works. A visible, specific verification process (ID, skills test, in-person check, references from known community members) would set a Cameroon entrant apart.
- OZOservir's field-first acquisition and cheap subscription (about US$3–8/month) is a plausible fit for Douala and Yaoundé, where artisans are price-sensitive. Its low 3–4% transaction fee reflects how hard it is to charge a high commission without a strong guarantee.

### Gaps
- No traction or funding data for any Cameroonian artisan platform. Facebook groups (e.g., "artisans Douala") could not be measured with the tools available.
- "Jobbing Cameroun" and the Cameroon "FixIt" app could not be verified. Jiji's Cameroon status is unconfirmed.
- No Fairwork rating exists for Cameroon (none found).

## Q2. African (non-Francophone) players: what they did and how they ended up

### Takeaway
The best African case study is **Lynk** (Kenya, 2015–2022). Its trust and quality approach was strong: in-person vetting, its own academy, standardized priced service packages ("SKUs") and M-PESA escrow-like payment. But high operating costs (transport, vetting and training, worker unreliability across too many categories) and a 10% take rate meant it wound down and was sold for parts to Eden Life in 2022. Eden Life then paused its consumer business in Kenya and Nigeria in Feb 2026. The successful African exit is **Kandua** (South Africa), a lead/fee marketplace with free business tools for pros. It reached about 40,000 vetted service companies and was acquired by insurer **Santam** (May 2024) for embedded insurance-claims and retail installation demand. **SweepSouth** shows that a managed, single-category cleaning model can raise capital ($11M in 2022), but it left Kenya at the end of 2022.

### Cited Findings
**Lynk (Kenya)** — Status: wound down, acquired by Eden Life (Apr 2022)
- Founded 2015 in Nairobi by Adam Grunewald (CEO) and Johannes Degn (CTO). Customers book via web or iOS/Android app. Pros use an Android Pro App, and SMS is used for job offers. Pros are found through vocational schools, referrals from customers and existing Pros, or direct application, and **vetted by in-person interview**. Lynk took a **10% administration fee** on payments via M-PESA or card. Customers pay upfront, and Pros are paid by M-PESA within 24 hours. Jobs are matched in 2–6 hours, and Pros have 15 minutes to reply Yes/No — [23]
- Traction: as of March 2019, more than 1,300 workers, more than 25,000 jobs, more than 2,200 customers and more than $2.5M paid to Pros. Quarterly jobs peaked at about 3,638 (Q4 2018). Funding included Safaricom Spark Fund (Sept 2017 seed) and a GSMA Innovation Fund grant (Feb 2018). A Pro testimonial says income doubled — [23]
- A Series A led by Lateral Capital was reported in July 2019 — [24]
- Early model (2016): about 400 verified workers across 64 categories. Profiles worked as the "LinkedIn for the LinkedOut" (work history, feedback, certificates, photos). The 10% commission was paid on top by the customer (a $10 job cost $11). Revenue to that date was about $40k, and the company was bootstrapped while raising $600k in seed — [25]
- Ex-COO Chris Maclay's account: customers would not pay for vetting and convenience alone. Lynk chose full service over lead generation. Open quoting (v1) suffered slow quotes, inconsistent and inaccurate pricing, and a cold-start bias toward Pros with the most ratings. Standardized fixed-price packages (v2) allowed a Pro within two hours and subsidized uniforms and tools that Pros repaid through work. Still, plumbing and electrical work were hard to standardize, and transport costs and distances were high. Pros were unreliable (example: a five-star Pro with 500 jobs vanished on job 501). Vetting and training costs were high, and vocational-school partnerships "never took off", so Lynk built its own Lynk Academy. Lesson: once services were standardized, customers trusted the *brand* more than any individual Pro — [26]
- At wind-down, Lynk had shut its website, stopped new services, completed pending orders and helped laid-off staff find jobs. Lifetime figures were about 50,000 jobs and $4.5M transferred to more than 2,000 workers. The Eden Life acquisition was brokered by Enza Capital (completed 28 Apr 2022) — [27][28]

**Eden Life (Nigeria → Kenya)** — Status: consumer (B2C) business paused in Kenya and Nigeria (Feb 2026); refocused on corporate clients; seeking capital to settle obligations — [29]

**Kandua (South Africa)** — Status: acquired by Santam (27 May 2024); operating as an independent subsidiary
- Founded 2014/2015 by Sayo Folawiyo and Arjun Khoosal. The platform is free for customers, and pros pay "a small fee for jobs they get". The algorithm matches each request to up to 5 interested pros, who send quotes. Customers "settle directly" with the pro. Pros get free tools: payments, quoting and invoicing, a website, reviews, insurance and training. Early 2021 figures: about 2,500 active pros, about 6,000 monthly gig opportunities and US$920k raised (Catalyst Fund/BFA portfolio) — [30]
- An unofficial description says contractors buy leads using platform credit — [31] (low confidence)
- July 2021 pre-Series A led by Knife Capital and Allan Gray E-Squared Ventures (amount undisclosed) — [32]
- At acquisition: "over 40,000 vetted home service companies" connected to about R50M of work per month. "Kandua for Business" serves retailers (installation added to goods purchases) and insurers (claims fulfilment). It replaced Santam's Home+, which closed on 30 June 2024. Santam stated an aim to reach informal artisans with training and financial inclusion — [33][34]
- Fairwork South Africa 2022 found Kandua among 6 platforms with clear contracts under South African law — [35]

**SweepSouth (South Africa; cleaning, with "Connect" for plumbing, electrical, handyman)** — Status: operating in SA; **left Kenya at end of 2022**
- Raised $11M led by Alitheia IDF (Sept 2022) for SA growth and planned Kenya, Nigeria and Egypt expansion — [36]
- The help centre says workers ("SweepStars") earn 80–96% of the booking fee, so SweepSouth keeps 4–20%. New workers start at 65% for 2–3 months to cover paid background and reference checks and onboarding — [37] (company-stated, via search summary)
- Fairwork Kenya 2022 scored SweepSouth 2/10 (Glovo and Little 4, Uber 2, Bolt 1) and noted "SweepSouth left the Kenyan market at the end of 2022" — [38]

**Other Kenya**
- **Fundis** (Nairobi): about 2,000 artisans, inspired by a craftsman shortage during a real-estate boom. It offers a "Fundi Mjanja" insurance and telemedicine benefit — [39][40] (via search summary)
- **Fixo** (launched Apr 2025, Nairobi pilot): "e-hailing for fundis", with **booking via a WhatsApp line**. Fundis are pre-screened and need security clearance. No pricing disclosed — [41]
- **Balozy** (claims 30 counties) [42]; **Ajira Yangu** (2024, pre-screened professionals) [43]; **Bingwa** (Nairobi app) [44]. Older entrants such as Workspap (2019) and SafiSafi (2016) appear inactive — [45]
- **Kazi Mtaani** is a government youth public-works programme, not a matching marketplace. I found no platform data (Wikipedia page missing).

**Nigeria**
- **Workclick** (Lagos, 2018): fixed listed prices, 20% commission, background screening plus company training before deployment — [46]
- **Clickafix** (2022): more than 10,000 Play Store downloads, backed by the Fast Forward venture studio — [47]
- **Fix All Africa** (Lagos, 2024): claims more than 2,200 certified artisans and "rigorous verification" (company claim) — [48]
- **Wrkman** (soft launch 1 Jul 2026): a "trust-led" relaunch campaign. Customer complaints it cites include delays, providers going silent after payment and no follow-up. It frames trust, not skills supply, as the core barrier — [49]
- **Viable247** (informal-services marketplace) — [50]. A BusinessDay summary notes that VConnect, KeyaHelp and Findworka "either failed or pivoted" — [50] (via search summary; reasons not given)
- **Gigmile** is adjacent, not a matching platform: lease-to-own vehicles for gig workers (8,500–10,000+ vehicles financed in Nigeria and Ghana), backed by Yango Ventures in Dec 2025, with IFC considering up to $4M in Sept 2026 — [51][52]

**Ghana**
- **Nsaboa**: 3-step verification (Ghana Card ID check, hands-on skills assessment, portfolio review). Mobile money (MoMo) payment is held until the customer confirms the job, and disputes are mediated. Free for both sides during launch — [53]
- **ProfArts** (GIZ-backed construction-artisan app) [54]; **GHartisans** (government Youth Employment Agency, "trained and certified artisans") [55]; **Ayuda** (Apr 2025; "premium" option with assessment and invoice, and "flex" option where the customer proposes a price) [56]; **Accra Handyman** (only known funding: MEST Scale incubator, Apr 2022) — [57]

### Inferences
- In Africa, the "managed / full-stack" model (Lynk, Eden, Homejoy-like) has repeatedly hit the cost wall: transport, vetting, training and worker reliability. The "lightweight marketplace plus SaaS for pros" model (Kandua) survived and found a strategic buyer, with B2B channels (insurers, retailers) supplying high-intent demand.
- Kandua's exit suggests a specific route for Cameroon: insurers (home and auto claims), property managers, telcos (e.g., an MTN/Orange MoMo merchant ecosystem, like Lynk's Safaricom/Masoko pilot) and appliance retailers need trusted artisans at scale and will pay for them.
- WhatsApp-first booking (Fixo) and community-vetted claims (Khidme) are emerging in 2025–26, which supports a WhatsApp/USSD-first channel for Cameroon.

### Gaps
- Kandua's actual lead price, credit packs and pro churn are not public. The Santam deal value is undisclosed.
- I found no dated official shutdown notice for KibaruaNow (Kenya). A search summary mentioned a 2019 shutdown, but I could not locate the primary source.
- Uganda and Rwanda artisan platforms were not covered (search budget exhausted).
- Lami (insurtech) and Workpay (HR/payroll) are not artisan-matching platforms. Not researched further.

## Q3. Global comparables: successes, pivots and failures

### Takeaway
At global scale, the winners took two very different paths. (a) **Lead-gen / directory** models that charge pros per lead or by subscription and stay out of the job (Thumbtack, Angi/HomeAdvisor, Bark, Checkatrade, GetNinjas). (b) **Heavily managed, standardized** models that train, equip and supply pros and take about 25–28% (Urban Company, IPO Sept 2025). Most failures were "Uber-for-X" managed models that grew through subsidies (Homejoy, Handy as a standalone, Kaodim, Housejoy's on-demand phase).

### Cited Findings
**Urban Company (India)** — Status: listed on NSE/BSE 17 Sept 2025
- Founded 2014 as UrbanClap, renamed 2020. Operates in India, UAE, Singapore, KSA (JV) and the US (2023). About 40,000 registered professionals (Mar 2025) — [58]
- FY25: revenue ₹1,144.5 cr (+38%), first full-year profit. 6.8M transacting users, about 48,000 average monthly active partners, average partner earnings ₹26,400/month — [59][60]
- Standard commission is 28% of service value, falling to about 27.5% for high-volume partners (per prospectus, via Finology) — [61] (search summary). Commissions and fees equalled 28.3% of professionals' average gross earnings in 9M FY26. Revenue also comes from selling tools and consumables to partners (the "B2B2C product" line) — [62]
- Quality control: training and certification, ratings, matching, availability rules, cancellation penalties, ID blocking. These triggered partner protests in 2023–24 over ID blocks, auto-assign and fees — [58][62]
- Q1 FY27: NTV ₹1,465 cr, operating revenue ₹528 cr (+44%), but a ₹92 cr net loss driven by InstaHelp (15-minute domestic help), with adjusted loss of ₹346 per order. The core India business remains profitable — [62]

**Thumbtack (US)** — Status: private, operating
- Founded 2008. Started as a directory to fix Craigslist's lack of verification and reviews. Raised about $698M in equity, valued at $3.2B (2021). About $400M revenue in FY2024 and about 300,000 active pros (June 2025). Partnerships with ChatGPT/OpenAI, Alexa and Redfin (2025) — [63]
- A Harvard case note says Thumbtack's "pay for leads" design limits disintermediation exposure, while TaskRabbit charged 30% on first transactions and 15% on repeats — [64]

**Angi / HomeAdvisor / Handy (US)** — Status: operating, shrinking
- Q3 2026: revenue $242M (−8.9%), service requests about 4M (−2.4%), leads 4.5M (−17.6%), active US pros about 101k (−14.4%), revenue per lead $47 (+9.8%). Revenue from large pros is up 20% and from small pros down 13%. About 300 roles cut. Unbranded organic search fell about two-thirds since 2023 because of Google changes — [65]
- "Homeowner Choice" (Jan 2025) ended auto-matching of network requests to pros. Network service requests fell from 0.9M to 0.6M year on year in Q1 2025 — [66]
- **Handy** (founded 2012, raised about $107M in disclosed rounds) was acquired by ANGI in Oct 2018. Angi's guidance noted "losses from Handy" — [67][68][69]

**TaskRabbit (US/EU)** — Status: operating under IKEA
- In 2014 it pivoted from an auction/bidding model to tasker-set rates, after US bids and completed tasks declined. In 2016 it moved to a 30% service fee plus a "Trust & Support" fee raised from 5% to 7.5%. IKEA acquired it in Sept 2017 and built assembly booking into IKEA purchases. Criminal background checks. "Happiness Pledge" covers up to $10,000 in damage, criticised for its exclusions. More than 200,000 workers — [70]

**UK lead-gen / directories**
- **Checkatrade**: vetting since 1998, "up to 12 checks" (ID, company history, CCJs, adverse media) with ongoing monitoring. Turned away a record 668 applicants in six months. Monthly subscription. Third-party sites also report per-lead and sponsored-listing fees, and sources conflict on this — [71]
- **Bark** (London): matches buyers to providers in the UK, US, CA, IE, AU, NZ, SG, ZA and IN. Bootstrapped. £20M turnover (2019), later about £70M revenue and £15M profit. Acquired by EMK Capital (2022, about £240M) — [72]

**GetNinjas (Brazil)** — Status: listed (B3: NINJ3), revenue declining
- Professionals buy virtual credits to unlock contact details of clients who posted requests. Pricing is dynamic: a lead costs less once other pros have bought it, and executives describe it as perishable, to be sold "hot" — [73]
- Revenue was R$14.03M in Q3 2024 (−10.8% YoY). TTM about R$60M — [74]. 1Q25 gross revenue was about 5% below 1Q24 because of "lower credit usage by service providers" and changes to monetization — [75]

**Southeast Asia / India failures**
- **Kaodim** (Malaysia; also Singapore, Philippines as Gawin, Indonesia as Beres): founded Nov 2014. Raised $4M Series A (2015) and $7M (2017). Instantly paired customers with a highly rated provider at a fixed bundled price. **Shut down 1 July 2022**, citing COVID lockdowns, labour shortages, rising costs and inflation hitting demand, fulfilment and margins — [76][77]
- **Housejoy** (India; Amazon-backed, about $30M raised): laid off staff and pulled back from 7 cities, refocusing on higher-margin beauty and handyman work [78]. From 2018 it moved into renovation and construction (average ticket about ₹60 lakh) — [79]
- **Babajob** (India blue-collar jobs) was acquired by Quikr in June 2017 after funding difficulties — [80][81]

**US failure: Homejoy** — Status: shut down 31 July 2015
- Founded 2010 (as Pathjoy). Raised about $38–40M (Google Ventures) and operated in more than 31 cities in the US, Canada and UK. It cited lawsuits over worker classification and "difficulties in maintaining profitability" — [82]
- $19 promotional cleanings, against a typical $85 market price. 75% of bookings came from discounts. Only about 25% of customers were still active after month 1 and under 10% after 6 months. Cleaners took clients off-platform. Quality fell as it expanded. Rival Handy pushed it into unprofitable offers — [83][84]

**Airtasker (Australia)**, the counter-example: slow, steady growth, cash-flow positive in late 2019 after cutting marketing, IPO in March 2021. It reduced labour risk by not controlling workers and by agreeing a deal with the NSW Labor Council — [84]

### Inferences
- Pure lead-gen businesses are now under structural pressure (Angi's falling pros and leads, GetNinjas' falling credit usage). Pros churn when leads don't convert, and SEO acquisition is being disrupted by AI search. In Cameroon, Google-SEO acquisition was never the main channel anyway; WhatsApp, Facebook and in-person channels are.
- Urban Company shows that a high take rate (about 28%) is achievable only when the platform adds clear value for the pro: steady demand, training, products and credit. It still causes labour friction.
- Every failure combined subsidised demand with low-frequency or low-margin services and fast geographic expansion.

### Gaps
- Amazon Home Services: I could not verify whether, or when, it was shut down or scaled back. A search found only an older expansion article [85]. Do not claim a shutdown without confirmation.
- Habitissimo (Spain/LatAm), Zaarly (US), MyBuilder and Rated People (UK), and Apna (India): not verified (search budget exhausted; Wikipedia pages missing).

## Q4. Disintermediation / leakage: evidence and countermeasures

### Takeaway
Leakage is real but often overstated. The strongest direct measurement (GPS data on a home-cleaning app) found almost none. Leakage is worst for recurring, relationship-based services (cleaning) where trust has already been established, and weakest for one-off, urgent or high-risk jobs (repairs). The deeper problem for a trust platform: rigorous research shows that **more trust signals can increase leakage**, so the platform has to keep supplying value *after* the first match (guarantee, payment protection, credit, re-matching, records), not just make the introduction.

### Cited Findings
- Astashkina, Bray, Momot & Salikhov (2023) studied 5,391 cleaners and 95,633 residences on a home-cleaning platform in a post-Soviet country with a shadow economy above 40% of GDP, and commissions of 20/30/40% depending on experience. They found "no more than one in 83 relationships" end in disintermediation. Suggested reasons: plentiful off-platform labour, safety value (entering strangers' homes), affluent customers who are price-insensitive (average cleaning $31.58), and the fact that 90% of cleaner–residence pairs did only one cleaning — [86]
- The same paper reviews Karacaoglu et al. (2022, a European cleaning platform), which estimated the platform "would enjoy about 24 percent more cleanings if not for disintermediation" (74% of customers leave after a cleaner's last job, against 66% after a typical cleaning) — [86]
- Gu & Zhu (2021, *Management Science*), an RCT on a freelance marketplace: extra trust signals increased hiring of high-quality freelancers, but "when the trust level is sufficiently high, it also increases disintermediation". Gu (2022): after China blocked Skype, detected disintermediation fell 18.7% and total charges rose 33.6% — [86][87]
- Hagiu & Wright (2024, *Management Science* 70(3)): partial leakage persists in equilibrium. Countermeasures modelled include investing in transaction benefits, limiting communication, referral fees, price-parity clauses and adding seller competition — [88]
- Thumbtack's per-lead pricing (pay upfront for the contact) makes leakage irrelevant to its revenue. TaskRabbit (around 2018) used a 30% first-transaction and 15% repeat fee and banned off-platform dealing in its terms — [64][89]
- Homejoy: clients and cleaners who built trust went off-platform. Scaled-and-Failed's lesson is that one-off repairs carry less leakage risk than recurring cleaning — [84]
- Industry analysis (Sacra) on why cleaning leaks: after the first visit, both sides have enough information to bypass the app — [90]
- Lynk: customers paid upfront via M-PESA before the job was matched, putting the platform in the payment flow — [23]
- Gurley: being in the payment flow is better. Billing suppliers afterward makes the platform feel like a "tax" — [91]
- Kandua lets customers "settle directly" with pros and charges pros per job/lead instead. This avoids fighting leakage on payment — [30]

### Inferences
- For Cameroon, charging for access or trust (a verification badge subscription, per-lead or per-contact fees, B2B contracts) is more robust than a high percentage commission on cash jobs that artisans can easily take off-platform.
- Countermeasures suited to Cameroon: (1) a workmanship guarantee or dispute mediation valid only on-platform; (2) a MoMo-held deposit, released on confirmation, for the first job and for larger jobs; (3) a portable, verifiable reputation record that artisans *want* to keep adding to (repeat jobs logged on-platform boost their score); (4) lower fees on repeat jobs, the opposite of TaskRabbit's 2016 move; (5) value-added services such as credit, insurance and tool financing (Lynk tool subsidies, Gigmile-style assets, Kandua insurance), which make staying on-platform worthwhile.
- Since repairs are mostly one-off and urgent, leakage may matter less than *supply reliability* (Lynk's vanishing Pros) and *price trust*.

### Gaps
- No African leakage measurement was found. The "35% WhatsApp leakage" figure for Dakar is an unverified vendor claim [19].
- NFX and a16z disintermediation essays were not retrieved (search budget exhausted).

## Q5. Verification approaches: offline agents, peer vouching, guarantees, background checks

### Takeaway
African platforms that took verification seriously did it **in person** (Lynk interviews and academy, Workclick screening plus training, Nsaboa skills test, Fixo security clearance, SweepSouth background and reference checks). It is expensive: Lynk named vetting and training costs as a structural burden. Global incumbents rely on document checks and ongoing monitoring (Checkatrade: up to 12 checks, with removals) plus guarantees (TaskRabbit up to $10k). Structured **peer vouching** is almost absent. Lynk sourced Pros through referrals from customers and existing Pros, and Khidme claims community verification, but no platform found uses formal vouching as its core trust mechanism, which is a white space.

### Cited Findings
- Lynk: in-person interview, recruitment via vocational schools and Pro/customer referrals, vetting per SKU, own Lynk Academy after vocational partnerships failed, a quality checklist on completion, and two-way ratings — [23][26]
- Workclick (Lagos): background screening to confirm competence plus company training before deployment — [46]
- Nsaboa (Ghana): Ghana Card ID, hands-on skills test, portfolio review, under one week, free to artisans — [53]
- Fixo (Kenya): pre-screening plus security clearance — [41]
- SweepSouth: paid background and reference checks, funded by a lower 65% payout during the first 2–3 months — [37]
- Whooti (Senegal): document checks, file validation, verified phone, references displayed. Certification planned — [15]
- OZOservir (Gabon): KYC planned. "Verified" badge sold in a paid Pro tier — [20]
- Checkatrade (UK): up to 12 checks, ongoing monitoring, members removed for failing standards, 668 rejected in six months — [71]
- TaskRabbit: criminal background checks. Happiness Pledge up to $10k, criticised for its exclusions — [70]
- Homejoy: background-checked and insured cleaners, yet quality still slipped at scale — [84]
- Kandua: "vetted" companies. Santam now provides insurer backing and claims-fulfilment demand — [33]
- Ghana government and donor schemes (GHartisans/YEA, GIZ ProfArts) certify artisans. This is an official credential layer a platform could plug into — [54][55]
- Caribou Digital: WhatsApp and Facebook groups are central to market-making and peer support for platform livelihoods in Kenya, Ghana, Nigeria and Uganda (700+ people studied). Nearly 90M women in four countries use WhatsApp to earn income — [92][93]

### Inferences
- A Cameroon design can combine cheap, scalable signals (phone and MoMo-name match, CNI photo, geotagged photos of past work) with **community vouching**: references from known patrons, quartier chefs, trade associations or *tontine* groups, weighted by the voucher's own reputation. In-person skills checks would be reserved for higher-tier badges. This avoids Lynk's flat, high cost of vetting everyone.
- Guarantees should be scoped tightly and funded (Santam-style insurer partnership, or a small fee pool). TaskRabbit's exclusions show that a vague guarantee damages trust.
- Ongoing monitoring with removals (Checkatrade) matters more than one-time vetting, given Lynk's "five-star Pro who vanished" problem.

### Gaps
- No African platform published costs or conversion for its vetting funnel.
- No platform found uses formal peer-vouching graphs at scale, so there are no outcome data on vouching.

## Q6. Take rates, lead fees, subscriptions: what they are and what effects were measured

### Takeaway
Observed models span roughly 3–28% commissions, per-lead credits, and flat subscriptions. In Africa, commissions of 10–20% are typical (Lynk 10%, Workclick 20%, SweepSouth 4–20% after onboarding). Global managed players run about 28% (Urban Company). Lead-gen players report falling volume when lead quality falls (Angi, GetNinjas). Measured effects are rare. The clearest are Angi's metrics after it ended auto-matching and GetNinjas' falling credit use.

- Measured effects: Angi's switch to homeowner choice cut network requests about 33% year on year (0.9M to 0.6M, Q1 2025) [66]. Leaving discounted legacy lead products and shifting to larger pros raised revenue per lead 9.8% while leads fell 17.6% and active pros 14.4% (Q3 2026) [65]
- GetNinjas: lower pro credit usage drove the year-on-year revenue decline in 1Q25 [75]
- TaskRabbit: the 2016 move to a 30% fee for all tasks (ending the 15% repeat rate) drew criticism that total fees can reach "70% of the original bid" [70]
- Homejoy: deep discounts brought in low-retention customers (25% retained at month 1) [83]
- Urban Company partners protested commissions, ID blocking and auto-assign in 2023–24 [58]

### Inferences
- High commissions in a cash, informal economy invite leakage and resentment. Fees tied to tangible value (verified badge, guaranteed payment, leads that convert, B2B contracts) are more defensible.
- A hybrid for Cameroon: free basic listing plus a cheap verified tier (OZOservir pricing as a benchmark) plus a small fee only on MoMo-protected jobs plus B2B contracts (insurers, property managers, NGOs, telcos). A first-job deposit is a better fit than a percentage on every repeat job.

### Gaps
- Kandua, Checkatrade and Thumbtack do not publish lead prices. No African A/B evidence on commission versus subscription was found.

## Q7. Post-mortems: why home-services marketplaces fail (investor and academic analyses)

### Takeaway
Recurring causes: (1) low purchase frequency, so customers rarely return; (2) heterogeneous, hard-to-standardize jobs, so quality and pricing are inconsistent; (3) high operating costs in managed models (transport, vetting, training, insurance); (4) buying demand with discounts, which fills the platform with low-retention users; (5) supply unreliability and churn; (6) fast geographic expansion before unit economics work; (7) labour-classification or regulatory exposure when the platform controls workers; (8) leakage of recurring relationships; (9) macro shocks (COVID for Kaodim).

### Cited Findings
- Gurley's 10 factors include fragmentation (positive), supplier sign-up friction (negative), **frequency** (failed marketplaces often target purchases that happen too rarely, and a good supplier match can end the need to return) and **payment flow** — [91]
- Lynk: standardization helped, but transport costs, balancing supply and demand across many categories, Pro reliability and vetting costs persisted. Customers wouldn't pay for vetting alone — [26]
- Homejoy: discounts, retention under 10% at 6 months, quality decline, 31-city expansion, classification lawsuits, leakage — [82][83][84]
- Kaodim: pandemic labour shortages and cost inflation squeezed margins in all four markets — [76][77]
- Housejoy: high customer acquisition costs, low margins, copycat startups. Retreated from 7 cities and moved to higher-ticket renovation and construction — [78][79]
- Eden Life: paused B2C in Kenya and Nigeria (2026), refocused on corporate clients — [29]
- Fora on the sector: on-demand cleaner Helpling posted multimillion-euro losses in the UK and Ireland — [68]
- Angi: weaker SEO and AI-search traffic, pro churn, falling leads (2025–26) — [65]
- Successful patterns: Kandua (tools for pros plus B2B, strategic exit) [30][33]; Airtasker (slow growth, cut marketing, low worker control) [84]; Urban Company (deep standardization plus product revenue) [62]

### Inferences
- A Cameroon trust platform should aim to be *infrastructure* (reputation plus verification plus payment protection) rather than a full-stack service company. It should start with the categories that recur most and are most trust-sensitive: electricians, plumbers, AC/fridge repair, phone repair, tailoring, domestic help. Expand to new cities only once retention is proven.
- B2B and B2B2C demand (insurers, property managers, NGOs, construction firms, telco and MoMo partners) can make up for low household frequency, as Kandua and Lynk's Masoko pilot both did.

### Gaps
- Bill Gurley's essay was retrieved. NFX, a16z and HBS case texts beyond the D3 student posts were not.

## Q8. Synthesis: concrete lessons for a Cameroon artisan trust and matching platform

### Takeaway
Build a lightweight, WhatsApp/MoMo-native **trust layer** (verified identity, community vouching, portable job history, protected first payment, a scoped guarantee). Don't build a capital-heavy managed service. Earn revenue from verification tiers, protected transactions and B2B contracts rather than a high commission. Acquire both sides in the field, quartier by quartier. Expect competition from the status quo (word of mouth, Facebook/WhatsApp, classifieds), not from any scaled incumbent.

### Inferences (lessons)
1. **Sell trust, not introductions.** Lynk found customers would not pay for vetting alone [26]. Gu & Zhu show trust signals alone can raise leakage [87]. The platform has to keep providing value after the match: MoMo-held payment, dispute handling, a guarantee, re-matching if the artisan doesn't turn up, warranties.
2. **Avoid the full-stack trap.** Lynk, Homejoy, Kaodim, Housejoy and Eden all ran into cost walls. Stay asset-light. Standardize only the simplest, highest-volume SKUs (e.g., socket installation, AC servicing, fridge regas), as Lynk's v2 did.
3. **Verification should be tiered and community-anchored.** Use cheap digital checks (CNI, phone and MoMo name match, photos of past work) plus structured vouching from known patrons and associations. Reserve paid in-person or skills checks for premium badges. Keep monitoring and remove bad actors (Checkatrade) [71].
4. **Channel: WhatsApp and USSD first, app second.** Fixo books via WhatsApp [41]. Caribou documents WhatsApp as the main livelihood channel [92]. Lynk used SMS Yes/No dispatch with a 15-minute reply window [23].
5. **Monetize lightly and defensibly.** A cheap verified-tier subscription (OZOservir benchmark 1,900–5,000 FCFA) [20]; a small fee on protected payments; flat first-contract fees (Whooti) [15]; B2B contracts. Avoid a high commission on cash repeat jobs.
6. **Find B2B demand early.** Insurers (Santam–Kandua) [33], retailers and installers (Lynk–Masoko, Kandua for Business) [23][33], property managers and NGOs give frequency and willingness to pay that households lack.
7. **Expand slowly by density.** Saturate a few Douala and Yaoundé quartiers before adding cities (Homejoy's 31-city sprawl versus Airtasker's slow growth) [84].
8. **Don't subsidize demand.** Homejoy's $19 cleanings kept 25% of customers after month 1 [83].
9. **Supply reliability is the real product.** Track punctuality and no-shows. Reward reliability with ranking and access to finance (tool, motorbike or phone financing, as with Lynk's tool subsidies and Gigmile) [26][51].
10. **Labour and dignity risks.** Urban Company's ID-blocking protests and the low Fairwork scores (SweepSouth 2/10) show that opaque deactivation and fee rules create backlash [38][58]. Publish rules and an appeals process from day one.

### Gaps
- Real Cameroon demand frequency, price points per trade, and the share of artisans using WhatsApp Business or MoMo merchant accounts were not established here (likely covered by other researchers).
- No outcome data exists for community vouching models at scale.
